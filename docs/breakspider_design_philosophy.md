# Breakspider Design Philosophy

## Project Thesis

**Breakspider is a personal corner of the internet first, and a portfolio second.**

It should feel less like an indie studio website and more like a highly customized internet profile: a place where professional work, game development, visual experiments, personal influences, odd little interactions, and long-term creative identity all coexist.

A useful shorthand is:

> **Breakspider is my profile for the internet as a whole — a forgotten digital sketchbook belonging to a software developer whose head is filled with fantasies, fostered by internet brainrot.**

The site should be expressive enough to feel personal and strange, but polished enough that its underlying engineering and design discipline are obvious.

---

## Identity and Purpose

Breakspider is not currently a separate studio persona with an established audience. For now:

- **The real-world professional identity comes first.**
- **Breakspider is the name and identity of the website itself.**
- Game development is a major focus, but the site should not read as a dedicated game studio homepage.
- Over time, if Breakspider becomes associated with released games, demos, or creative work, the brand can become more prominent.

The site should function simultaneously as:

1. A professional portfolio.
2. A game-development hub.
3. A long-term personal creative archive.
4. An interactive playground.
5. A public-facing internet profile.

The long-term goal is not to “finish a portfolio site,” but to build a place that can continue accumulating new work, ideas, creatures, experiments, and references over time.

---

## Core Personality

The intended emotional tone is:

- **Cozy**
- **Chaotic**
- **Playful**
- **Professional underneath the mess**

The site should feel handmade and personal rather than corporate or sanitized.

The preferred metaphor is not quite a scrapbook. It is closer to **opening an old sketchbook you forgot about**:

- finished pieces next to half-finished ideas,
- notes in margins,
- things taped or pasted in,
- doodles and experiments,
- artifacts from different moments,
- and occasional things you do not remember putting there.

A second important metaphor is a **deeply customized personal profile or computer environment**.

Together, those metaphors define the site's tone better than “retro website” or “old-web portfolio.”

---

## Internet-Cultural Lineage

Breakspider should draw from the internet culture actually experienced by its creator rather than generic web nostalgia.

The strongest reference period begins around **2007 and later**.

Primary influences include:

- OG YouTube profiles
- Gaia Online
- Tumblr
- RP forums
- Forum signatures
- Avatars
- Highly customized user profiles
- CSS-heavy profile layouts
- Art trades
- Favorites lists
- Featured songs/videos
- Badges
- Titles
- Collectibles
- Profile customization
- Small communities with visible user identity

The site should **not** become generic GeoCities/Y2K nostalgia bait for eras or subcultures that were not personally meaningful.

The goal is not to recreate the old web literally. The goal is to preserve what made those spaces appealing:

- ownership,
- customization,
- visible personality,
- identity through layout,
- discoverability,
- little rewards,
- and the sense that a page belonged to a specific person.

---

## “Chaos With a Purpose”

This is one of the site's central design rules.

The target is roughly **6/10 maximalism**: clearly busy, layered, and expressive, but still readable and intentionally composed.

The site should feel like:

> **chaos with a purpose, not purpose surrounded by chaos.**

The composition may include:

- overlapping elements,
- varied scale,
- layered imagery,
- stickers,
- sprites,
- custom interface panels,
- dense visual clusters,
- taped/pinned/sketched elements,
- motion,
- and playful spatial relationships.

But every meaningful page should still preserve visual hierarchy.

A useful test for any design element is:

> **Does this reveal something, reward exploration, communicate information, or create an interesting interaction?**

If the answer is no, it may only be decoration.

Examples:

**Good**
- A sprite that awards a hidden cursor cosmetic when clicked.
- A creature wandering around the page and reacting to interaction.
- An influence collage that visually connects personal taste to creative work.
- A profile widget whose content changes with current priorities.

**Less useful**
- Random GIFs added only because “old websites had GIFs.”
- Visual clutter that makes important information harder to find.
- Effects that imitate nostalgia without saying anything personal.

---

## Two Layers of Experience

Breakspider should support two very different kinds of visitor.

### The 30-Second Visitor

A hiring manager or recruiter should quickly understand:

- who the creator is,
- that they are a full-stack software engineer and game developer,
- their professional strengths,
- what kind of work they have done,
- that they are currently available for work when relevant,
- and how to contact them.

Nothing professionally important should require discovery.

### The 20-Minute Visitor

Someone willing to explore should gradually discover:

- game projects,
- familiars,
- design notes,
- interactions,
- collectibles,
- interests,
- references,
- odd jokes,
- hidden pages,
- minigames,
- art,
- strange interface details,
- and the underlying personality of someone with roughly two decades of internet experience.

The deeper the visitor explores, the more personal the site should become.

---

## Navigation Philosophy

Navigation should be:

- persistent,
- minimal,
- obvious enough to use,
- and focused on essential information.

Not every page should be exposed in global navigation.

The important professional and structural destinations should be easy to access, while more personal or playful areas can be reached contextually or through exploration.

The site should have a **larger sitemap than navbar**.

Hidden things should be fun, optional, and non-essential.

A visitor should never have to solve a puzzle to find a résumé, project history, or contact information.

---

## The Interchangeable Spotlight

The homepage should contain a prominent **Spotlight** or centerpiece whose content can change depending on current priorities.

This is a core structural requirement.

Examples:

### Current job-search state
The spotlight may emphasize:

- name,
- professional identity,
- availability,
- full-stack engineering experience,
- game-development interests,
- résumé/contact links.

### Future game-development state
The spotlight may feature:

- the current game project,
- a trailer,
- a newly created familiar,
- a demo,
- a dev update.

### Future experimental state
The spotlight may feature:

- a new interactive toy,
- a site experiment,
- a special release,
- a new collection or feature.

The homepage should not need redesigning every time the site's current priority changes.

---

## Professional Positioning

The intended professional impression is:

> **An excellent developer with unusually strong design instincts.**

The site should not frame its creator primarily as a visual designer who also codes.

The strongest professional foundation is real full-stack web application engineering experience, especially in areas such as:

- digital marketing software,
- contact management,
- multimedia management,
- generative-AI-assisted workflows,
- frontend application development,
- backend systems,
- application architecture,
- integrations,
- QA,
- deployment,
- debugging,
- and product support.

The near-term job-search goal is broad, but with a temporary emphasis on work adjacent to the game industry, including:

- game development,
- game QA,
- tools,
- game-related web development,
- interactive experiences,
- and other gaming-adjacent software roles.

The site should communicate professional credibility without pretending that existing web/software experience is already equivalent to years of game-industry experience.

---

## Professional Differentiator

The most important unusual professional trait to communicate is:

> **A love of unique interactions and system interconnectivity, especially systems that people enjoy discovering rather than merely using.**

This should not only be stated in copy.

The site itself should demonstrate it.

Examples include:

- collectibles,
- hidden interactions,
- persistent customization,
- interconnected content,
- unlockable UI behavior,
- familiar profiles,
- evolving site states,
- and interactions that reveal more about the site's creator.

The portfolio should act as proof of the underlying philosophy.

---

## Project Presentation Philosophy

Project pages should avoid sterile portfolio case-study templates.

They should feel more like **opening a section of the sketchbook dedicated to one project**.

Project pages may combine:

- screenshots,
- animated GIFs or video,
- diagrams,
- code snippets,
- design notes,
- abandoned ideas,
- prototypes,
- technical explanations,
- visual annotations,
- architecture notes,
- short reflections,
- and polished summaries.

They should still communicate the same useful information as a conventional case study:

- What problem existed?
- What was the creator's role?
- What constraints mattered?
- What was built?
- Why was it built that way?
- What technical or design challenges were interesting?
- What was the outcome?
- What was learned?

The information should be professional, but the presentation should stay expressive.

---

## Writing and Case Studies

Long-form professional documentation is not expected to be a natural strength.

The site should therefore support a modular writing process.

A useful content pattern is:

**Problem → Constraint → Solution → Why → Challenge → Result**

This can be developed from conversational notes and later polished into finished copy.

The goal is to preserve clear reasoning without forcing every project page into a formal essay.

---

## Familiar Catalogue Philosophy

A prominent long-term feature should be a **Pokédex-like catalogue of familiars**, while maintaining an original visual identity.

The catalogue should include every familiar with enough finished custom visual work to justify a public entry.

Selecting a familiar may reveal:

- sprite or animation,
- name,
- description,
- personality/flavor,
- fighting style,
- role,
- mechanics,
- associated statuses,
- appearances in games,
- design notes,
- and other contextual information.

The catalogue should not be limited conceptually to one game.

Over time, it can become a broader archive of creatures made across multiple Breakspider projects.

This makes it both:

- a game-development showcase,
- and a long-term piece of the site's identity.

---

## Collectibles and Visitor Progression

Collectibles are not merely decorative jokes.

They should form a lightweight **visitor progression and customization system**.

Visitors may unlock things such as:

- badges,
- alternate cursor styles,
- cursor followers,
- profile decorations,
- site themes,
- interactive toys,
- hidden icons,
- visual effects,
- and other cosmetic or experiential rewards.

Unlock methods may include:

- finding hidden sprites,
- clicking unusual objects,
- solving riddles,
- answering trivia,
- completing minigames,
- interacting with page elements in unexpected ways,
- and discovering secret pages.

Collectibles may reference things the creator loves.

This allows personal influences to appear through interaction instead of through explicit lists like “favorite games.”

The collectible system should feel real and internally consistent even if it is not tied to an account.

---

## Persistence Philosophy

For the initial version:

- Visitor progress can be stored locally in the browser.
- No account is required.
- Unlocks, equipped cosmetics, badges, themes, and similar state can persist locally.

Losing browser storage may lose progress in the early version.

A future extension may allow optional email-based backup/restore, potentially combined with newsletter signup or other lightweight account functionality.

This should not be necessary for v0.1.

---

## Discoverable Content

Hidden content is encouraged when it adds personality.

Potential examples:

- secret pages,
- jokes,
- small gags,
- strange interactive rooms,
- unlocks,
- minigames,
- puzzles,
- references,
- hidden characters,
- and experimental interfaces.

The important rule is:

> **Hidden content may deepen the site, but must never contain information that a professional visitor needs in order to understand the creator's experience or contact them.**

---

## Sketchbook / Devlog Philosophy

Updates should be **recurring but not regular**.

There should be no artificial posting cadence.

Posts should appear when there is genuinely something worth talking about, such as:

- a new mechanic,
- a visual experiment,
- a familiar design,
- an implementation problem,
- a prototype,
- a release,
- a design decision,
- or an interesting technical discovery.

The section should feel like irregular sketchbook entries rather than a content-marketing blog.

---

## Social Features

The site is primarily about exploring one person's internet profile.

Social or user-generated features may exist later, but should remain constrained.

Possible future ideas include:

- a guestbook,
- small drawings,
- simple reactions,
- visitor messages,
- or other lightweight ways for visitors to leave traces.

Any user-generated content system must be designed around moderation and abuse resistance.

The goal is not to build a general-purpose social network or forum.

---

## Animation and Interaction

Breakspider should contain a high amount of animation and interaction.

Potential interaction styles include:

- draggable objects,
- animated sprites,
- responsive decorative elements,
- hover reactions,
- click reactions,
- changing status text,
- unlockable interface behavior,
- simulated profile widgets,
- profile-like windows,
- cursor followers,
- persistent cosmetic changes,
- and small minigames.

Interaction should support the site's themes of curiosity and discovery.

The site should not become an interaction obstacle course.

Essential controls should remain obvious and reliable.

---

## Audio Philosophy

Audio is welcome, especially:

- UI sounds,
- collectible jingles,
- small interaction sounds,
- notification-style cues,
- and possibly light ambient sound in the future.

Background music is not a priority and may be too intrusive.

Most importantly:

> **Audio must always be easy to mute from anywhere on the site.**

The site should provide a clear persistent audio toggle.

Users should never need to mute the browser tab because the site's own controls are hidden or incomplete.

Audio state should persist.

---

## Visual Asset Philosophy

Original assets are encouraged whenever practical.

The site can become a place to practice and develop:

- Aseprite work,
- custom sprites,
- icons,
- UI elements,
- creature art,
- decorative graphics,
- and profile-style visual assets.

References to outside media may sometimes be appropriate, but custom reinterpretation is preferred when it better supports the site's identity.

The site should feel authored rather than assembled entirely from ripped source material.

---

## Mobile Philosophy

Desktop should initially be the canonical Breakspider experience.

The desktop layout can be more layered, spatial, overlapping, and profile-like.

Mobile should:

- remain fully usable,
- preserve the personality,
- keep essential content accessible,
- simplify the composition where necessary,
- and avoid forcing a desktop collage into a narrow viewport.

The mobile version does not need to reproduce every desktop interaction exactly.

Long-term, mobile may gain its own exclusive Easter eggs or interactions rather than being treated only as a reduced desktop experience.

---

## Content Authoring Philosophy

Not every kind of content should require editing source code.

A useful rule is:

> **Content gets authored. Experiences get programmed.**

Content-heavy systems that should eventually have authoring tools include:

- familiars,
- sketchbook/devlog entries,
- possibly spotlight content,
- and other frequently updated structured data.

For example, adding a familiar should eventually be a workflow like:

1. Upload sprite/art.
2. Enter name.
3. Write description.
4. Add fighting style/role/mechanics.
5. Add tags.
6. Publish.

By contrast, a new hidden puzzle or custom collectible interaction may reasonably require touching code.

---

## Technical Philosophy

The technical stack should support rapid shipping and highlight the creator's strongest skills.

The current preferred baseline is:

- **Next.js**
- **TypeScript**
- **Firebase**

Potential Firebase uses include:

- Firestore for structured content,
- Storage for uploaded assets,
- Auth for private authoring tools,
- and future optional visitor accounts if ever needed.

There is no need to switch frameworks merely to demonstrate novelty.

Going outside the comfort zone is valuable only if it meaningfully improves the site.

Libraries are welcome where they solve established problems well.

Custom code should be reserved for interactions that are genuinely unique to Breakspider.

The goal is not to reinvent dragging, animation, storage, or routing unnecessarily.

The engineering ambition should go toward the site's unusual behavior and interconnected systems.

---

## Source Code Philosophy

The site source can be public.

This is desirable because the site itself should function as a portfolio project.

A public repository can demonstrate:

- frontend architecture,
- component design,
- interaction systems,
- animation logic,
- responsive implementation,
- content architecture,
- accessibility considerations,
- persistence,
- and engineering discipline.

---

## v0.1 Philosophy

The first release should be useful quickly.

The site should not remain private for months while every playful system is developed.

A good v0.1 should prove:

1. The visual identity works.
2. The site is professionally useful.
3. The project can support future expansion.
4. The discovery philosophy is real.

Likely v0.1 priorities include:

- a distinctive homepage,
- the professional spotlight,
- an “Available for Work” state,
- clear professional background,
- résumé/LinkedIn/contact access,
- at least one major game project,
- a basic familiar catalogue if enough assets are ready,
- sketchbook infrastructure,
- responsive mobile handling,
- a persistent mute control,
- and a small first set of collectibles.

A handful of collectibles is enough to establish the system.

The initial release does **not** require:

- dozens of collectibles,
- complex themes,
- cloud saves,
- full visitor accounts,
- elaborate minigames,
- guestbook drawing tools,
- background music,
- or a complete hidden-site ecosystem.

The intended first impression is:

> “Oh, this site is weird.”

Then:

> “Oh wow, this is actually really polished.”

Then:

> “Wait, I can click that?”

---

## Anti-Goals

Breakspider should not become:

- A corporate studio website.
- A generic indie-game landing page.
- LinkedIn with pixel borders.
- A conventional three-card developer portfolio.
- A sterile UX case-study collection.
- Nostalgia bait for internet eras the creator did not experience.
- A purely ironic parody of old websites.
- A website that is difficult to navigate for the sake of being strange.
- A collection of effects with no relationship to identity or content.
- A social network.
- A blog with an artificial publishing schedule.
- A project that cannot launch because every future feature must exist first.

---

## Design Review Questions

When evaluating a new feature, layout, interaction, or visual idea, ask:

1. **Does this feel personal rather than generic?**
2. **Does this belong to the creator's actual internet history?**
3. **Does it reveal something, reward exploration, communicate information, or create a worthwhile interaction?**
4. **Does it preserve “chaos with a purpose”?**
5. **Can a professional visitor still understand the important information immediately?**
6. **Does the deeper version reward someone who keeps exploring?**
7. **Does this demonstrate strong engineering or design instincts rather than only adding decoration?**
8. **Is this content that should be authored, or an experience that should be programmed?**
9. **Does this belong in v0.1, or is it something Breakspider can grow into later?**
10. **Would this still feel like Breakspider if the nostalgic styling were removed?**

That final question is especially important.

The site should be defined by its personality, systems, and approach to interaction — not only by visual references to older internet aesthetics.
